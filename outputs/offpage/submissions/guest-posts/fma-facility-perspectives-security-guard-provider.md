## 3. Article draft  *(≈900 words — educational, AU English; no fabricated statistics)*

### What Facility Managers Should Look For in a Security Guard Provider

Choosing a security guard provider is one of the higher-stakes decisions a facility manager makes. Get it right and you reduce risk, satisfy compliance, and free your team to focus on the building. Get it wrong and you inherit missed patrols, slow responses, and a reporting black hole — usually discovered only after an incident. Yet most providers look similar on paper. Here is a practical checklist for separating a genuine security partner from a commodity guarding contract.

**1. Licensing and compliance — ask for evidence, not assurances.** Security is a licensed industry, and requirements differ by state and territory. A credible provider holds the relevant state security licences, carries current public liability, professional indemnity and workers' compensation insurance, and can produce certificates of currency on request. Membership of the Australian Security Industry Association (ASIAL) is a useful signal of professional standards. Don't accept "we're fully compliant" — ask to see it.

**2. Verifiable performance — can they prove the guard was there?** This is the question most contracts can't answer. Traditional guarding runs on trust: you assume patrols happened. Modern providers replace trust with proof. NFC patrol verification, for example, records a tamper-evident, time-stamped check at each patrol point, so you can see exactly when and where a guard attended. If a provider can't show you proof-of-presence, you're buying a promise, not a service.

**3. Real-time visibility and reporting.** You shouldn't have to wait for a monthly summary to know what happened on your site. The better providers give clients live visibility — incident reports, photos and patrol logs available in real time through a reporting app. This matters for two reasons: it lets you act on issues immediately, and it gives you the audit trail compliance and insurers increasingly expect.

**4. Monitoring and response integration.** Guards are most effective when they sit inside a wider security chain. Look for a provider whose guarding integrates with alarm monitoring and rapid alarm response — ideally backed by a Grade A1 monitoring centre (aligned to AS/NZS 2201.2:2022). End-to-end detect-verify-respond is far stronger than a standalone guard, and it removes the finger-pointing that happens when monitoring and guarding are different vendors.

**5. National coverage and multi-site consistency.** If you manage sites across states, a single provider with genuine national coverage delivers consistent service levels, one point of accountability, and uniform reporting — instead of stitching together regional contractors with different standards. Ask how they guarantee consistency across locations.

**6. Training, vetting and duty of care.** Licensing is the floor, not the ceiling. Ask how guards are vetted and trained, particularly for de-escalation and occupational-violence response — critical in healthcare, education and public-facing sites. The quality of the person on the ground is ultimately what protects your people and assets.

**7. Procurement and tender fit.** If your security goes to tender, your provider should speak procurement's language: clear SLAs and KPIs (response times, patrol completion, incident reporting), evidence of probity, and a capability statement you can drop into a submission. A provider who makes your tender easier is a provider worth shortlisting.

**The common thread.** Notice what links these points: the best providers make security *verifiable* and *visible*. They don't ask you to take patrols, responses and compliance on faith — they prove them. As you review your next contract or RFP, weight these criteria heavily. The cheapest guard hour is rarely the lowest-risk one, and the provider who can show you what happened on your site is worth more than the one who simply tells you.
